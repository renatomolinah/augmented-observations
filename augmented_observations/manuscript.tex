\begin{abstract}
\begin{normalsize}
\begin{singlespace}
What are the socioeconomic returns to augmented aircraft observations in hurricane forecasting? We study this question by linking data-denial experiments for eight US hurricane landfalls (2017–2020) to a structural model of hurricane costs, isolating the economic returns of in-situ reconnaissance. We find that augmented observations reduce average forecast uncertainty by 0.26 m/s at the 3-day lead time averaged over all counties affected by hurricane-force winds. Implementing this improvement into a valuation framework, we estimate that this precision gain generates over \$23 million in social value per affected county. Given the relative operating costs of reconnaissance, these gains yield a high benefit-to-cost ratio. Ultimately, our findings demonstrate that strategic investment in the NOAA aircraft fleet delivers outsized economic returns in terms of protection against hurricanes.
\end{singlespace}
\end{normalsize}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Landfalling hurricanes are among the costliest natural disasters in the United States, causing catastrophic property damage and significant loss of life along the coast each season. One of the key levers for mitigating these impacts is forecasting. Accurate predictions of a storm's track and intensity allow households and government agencies to make better protective decisions in the short window between a hurricane's formation and its landfall. These forecasts, in turn, are only as reliable as the observations that initialize the numerical models behind them. Among the most valuable of these inputs are augmented aircraft observations (AO): high-resolution, in-situ atmospheric data collected by reconnaissance aircraft, the \emph{Hurricane Hunters}, and assimilated by the National Hurricane Center (NHC) within the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA).

The meteorological value of these observations is well documented. Decades of ``data denial'' experiments, which compare forecasts run with and without aircraft data, consistently demonstrate reductions in track and intensity errors \citep{Majumdar2016}. However, far less is known about their socioeconomic return. Translating improved forecast accuracy into avoided damages and avoided casualties is essential for justifying the substantial cost to the taxpayer of maintaining and modernizing the reconnaissance fleet, yet the existing evidence base remains thin and largely qualitative.

Recent shifts in federal funding priorities make a rigorous understanding of the economic benefit of weather forecasting even more pressing. For example, the FY2026 budget request proposed substantial reductions to NOAA's primary research offices \citep{CRS2026NOAABudget}, while broader reduction-in-force directives \citep{EO14210Workforce2025} led to staffing adjustments at the National Weather Service and the NHC, directly affecting routine upper-air observations. Similarly, proposed cuts to the National Science Foundation created operational uncertainty at the National Center for Atmospheric Research, a central hub for forecast modeling and data assimilation \citep{NSF2025BudgetRequest, Science2025NCAR}. Although Congress subsequently restored much of this funding and mitigated structural changes \citep{witze2026world}, this ongoing budget volatility highlights a critical gap. Determining the optimal level of investment in forecasting infrastructure requires robust, quantifiable measures of the socioeconomic value these systems provide. Such measures are crucial for a transparent discussion of the value of maintaining publicly funded research and operations.

In this paper, we fill this gap and quantify the social value of AO by linking the physical forecast improvements they deliver to the economic costs of hurricanes. Our empirical strategy proceeds in three stages. We first estimate the cost of forecast error from a county-by-storm panel of forecast and realized hurricane damages covering the 37 hurricanes that made landfall in the continental United States between 2005 and 2024. We then estimate the precision gain attributable to AO from recent data-denial experiments for a subset of eight continental-US landfalls between 2017 and 2020, including Hurricanes Harvey, Irma, and Michael, among the costliest hurricanes on record after Katrina. Finally, we integrate these physical and economic estimations to derive a dollar-denominated valuation of AO, establishing a quantitative benchmark for future program evaluation and infrastructure investments.

We find that the social cost of forecast uncertainty is concentrated entirely in high-intensity events. For counties exposed to hurricane-force winds ($\geq33$ m/s), a one-unit increase in the squared demeaned wind-speed forecast error raises total costs by approximately \$5.03 million, while the effect for weaker exposures is small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. AO, in turn, reduce the ex ante 3-day wind-speed forecast standard deviation by approximately 0.26 m/s. Monetizing this precision gain through an ex ante valuation framework yields a benefit of roughly \$23.6 million per affected county-storm, and approximately \$1.46 billion in retrospective social value across the storms that implemented AO between 2017 and 2020. Extending this valuation to every hurricane-force, 3-day-lead county-storm in the 2005--2024 forecast panel, over which augmented observations were flown for essentially every landfalling hurricane, raises the cumulative retrospective value to approximately \$5.2 billion (Appendix \ref{app:per-storm}).

These benefits are substantial relative to the costs of the program. Set against the \$1.4 billion acquisition cost to recapitalize NOAA's aging WP-3D Orion fleet with new C-130J Super Hercules aircraft \citep{GAO2025HurricaneHunter}, the benefits accrued over just a four-year window recover the entire fleet investment. This estimated return is also several times larger than the closest official benchmark, the 2016 Analysis of Alternatives commissioned by NOAA \citep{ANSER2016ExternalBenefits}, a difference largely driven by our framework's ability to capture the economic value of reducing ex ante forecast uncertainty, which previous assessments omit.

Overall, our approach draws on two literatures. The first is the rich body of data-denial experiments \citep{Franklin_DeMaria1992, BurpeeEA1996, MajumdarEA2013, Majumdar2016, SippelEA2022, DitchekEA2023}, which isolate the contribution of AO to forecast precision. The second is the growing economics of weather forecasts \citep{shrader2026weather, molina2024social}, from which we adopt the cost-loss value-of-information framework of \citet{molina2024social} that maps forecast precision into expected hurricane costs.

Against this background, this paper contributes to the literature in three fundamental ways. First, we provide the first nationwide economic valuation of a direct forecasting asset. A growing body of work values weather forecasts by the protective decisions they inform, across hurricanes \citep{molina2024social}, temperature and mortality \citep{shrader2023fatal, shrader2026weather}, precipitation and labor \citep{downey2023rain}, advisory lead time and traffic crashes \citep{anand2025forecasts}, and the closely related value of environmental information \citep{barwick2024fog}. However, this literature largely treats the forecasting system as a black box, pricing the final output while taking the infrastructure that produces it as given. Where previous work, such as \citet{molina2024social}, values aggregate historical improvements in hurricane forecasts, we isolate and price a single, budgetable input. This is an important empirical advance, as the upstream input margin is where actual federal funding and procurement decisions are made. 

Second, we introduce a generalizable framework for valuing scientific observation systems. Estimating the socioeconomic value of information is notoriously difficult because the protective actions it induces are rarely fully observed \citep{molina2024social, shrader2026weather}. We overcome this barrier by linking two observable quantities: the \emph{ex ante} forecast variance removed by a specific instrument, identified from meteorological data-denial experiments \citep{DitchekEA2023}, and the marginal cost of that variance in realized hurricane damages, identified from a structural cost-loss model \citep{molina2024social}. The approach requires no micro-level behavioral data, and it extends to any monitoring system whose physical contribution can be experimentally isolated, complementing recent efforts to price information systems from pollution disclosure \citep{barwick2024fog} to seasonal climate outlooks \citep{lemoine2024financial}. 

Third, we provide an empirically grounded tool to guide active public investment and climate adaptation. Tropical cyclones impose large and persistent economic costs \citep{hsiang2014causal, deryugina2017fiscal}, and climate change is expected to raise both their destructiveness \citep{mendelsohn2012impact, kossin2020global} and the difficulty of forecasting them \citep{emanuel2017will}. Information is one margin of adaptation, alongside physical capital such as seawalls \citep{fried2022seawalls}, yet investment in the systems that produce it has been guided by assumption-based accounting \citep{ANSER2016ExternalBenefits}. Replacing that accounting with a dollar-denominated valuation, we show that upstream investment in data acquisition yields substantial downstream returns, giving policymakers a transparent benchmark that shifts the discussion from the upfront cost of infrastructure to its realized public value.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section \ref{sec:background} provides background on hurricane forecasting, the reconnaissance program, and the data-denial experiments that identify the value of aircraft observations. Section \ref{sec:model} presents the cost-loss model that underpins our valuation. Section \ref{sec:data} describes our two data sources, and Section \ref{sec:methods} details the three-stage empirical strategy. Section \ref{sec:results} presents the results and the cost-benefit analysis, and Section \ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with policy implications.

\section{Background} \label{sec:background}

\subsection{Hurricane Forecasting} \label{sec:forecasting}

A hurricane is a tropical cyclone with maximum sustained winds of at least 33 m/s, and its destructive potential rises steeply with intensity, which the Saffir-Simpson scale summarizes in five categories \citep{Emanuel2003}. The resulting damage is multidimensional, arising from wind, inland flooding, and coastal storm surge, but wind speed remains the central measure of intensity and the basis for official watches and warnings \citep{CBO2019, HilderbrandXie2025}. Throughout an active storm, the NHC issues forecasts of its track and intensity every six hours, each pairing a single deterministic prediction with probabilistic guidance that conveys the uncertainty around it \citep{RappaportEA2009, Broad2007}. These forecasts give households and public agencies the information they need to allocate protective resources in the short window before landfall \citep{molina2024social}.

The skill of these forecasts has improved markedly over recent decades, though unevenly \citep{AlakaJr2024}. Track prediction has advanced the most, while intensity, and especially rapid intensification near landfall, has remained substantially harder to forecast \citep{Marks_Shay1998, Resnick2018, Enten2017, Norcross2018}. Much of the improvement followed a sustained national investment in forecast research and operations through the Hurricane Forecast Improvement Program, which since 2007 has reduced wind-speed forecast errors at a far faster rate than in prior decades \citep{GallEA2013, MarksEA2019, molina2024social}. These gains are increasingly consequential, as climate change is expected to raise the destructiveness of tropical cyclones \citep{mendelsohn2012impact, kossin2020global} while making their behavior harder to predict \citep{emanuel2017will}.

Forecast skill ultimately rests on the observations that initialize the numerical models behind each prediction \citep{HamillEA2012}. These models assimilate vast streams of satellite, surface, and in-situ data, yet the core of a hurricane sits over open ocean where conventional observations are sparse. Closing that gap is the role of aircraft reconnaissance, the observing system whose economic value we quantify and to which we now turn.

%      Institutional Background

\subsection{Aircraft Reconnaissance}

In-situ measurements inside a hurricane are difficult and expensive to collect. To gather vital data in and around life-threatening hurricanes in the Atlantic, hurricane reconnaissance flights began in the mid-1940s, flown by the US Navy and Air Force. By the mid-1950s, routine reconnaissance flights were also conducted by the US Weather Bureau’s National Hurricane Research Project (NHRP), the precursor to what is now the Hurricane Research Division (HRD), operated by NOAA. HRD has been conducting operational and research flights into and around hurricanes since 1982, and the Air Force's history of flying into hurricanes began nearly four decades prior \citep{Sheets1990,Marks_Shay1998}.

However, not all hurricanes have reconnaissance flights into and around them. The NHC within NOAA is the primary decision-maker of what and when to fly \citep{OFCM2024,RappaportEA2009}. Specialists at NHC are tasked with identifying if a tropical cyclone has formed, how intense it is, how large it is, and other vital statistics. They are also tasked with forecasting a storm's future track and intensity, which is aided when aircraft reconnaissance data are provided to the forecast models. Priority for resources is given to proximity to land, potential for significant impacts on land, and challenging environmental conditions that increase forecast uncertainty \citep{Aberson2003, Majumdar2016}. Once a reconnaissance flight is requested, coordination between NHC, the US Air Force, and HRD determines what planes will fly, where they will fly, and what instruments need to be available \citep{OFCM2024}. All landfalling hurricanes (Category 1 and above) between 2005 and 2024 had aircraft reconnaissance flights.

Aside from flight-level measurements of wind speed, estimates of the storm's central pressure, and radar mapping of the storm's structure, a key instrument over the past four decades has been the dropwindsonde (often called a ``dropsonde"). This is an instrument package released from an aircraft that collects high-resolution in-situ data along a vertical profile of the atmosphere down to the ocean surface as it falls slowly with the aid of a parachute \citep{Hock_Franklin1999}.

These data are collected and processed by forecast models in real-time. In principle, providing more data to a model will improve its ability to accurately represent and forecast the atmosphere. This process is called data assimilation (DA) and it is a fundamental and complex step in all of numerical weather prediction \citep{Kalnay2003, Bannister2017}. Providing a hurricane model with information about the intensity, structure, and surrounding environment of the hurricane should, in general, make that model more accurate.

\subsection{Data Denial Experiments}

One of the earliest studies to document the impact of dropsonde observations on hurricane track forecast models was \citet{Franklin_DeMaria1992}. They utilized data from fourteen Atlantic hurricane surveillance flights spanning 1982-1989 and conducted data denial experiments in a research-grade model. In a data denial experiment, a model is run with and without a specific dataset, then the results are validated against the observations, and relative improvements or degradations are calculated. In this case, when including dropsonde data, the overall reduction in track error was approximately 16\% in the critical 24-36-hour lead times, with a positive impact at all lead times.

\citet{BurpeeEA1996} provides the first robust accounting of a data denial study using an operational forecast model. This experiment utilized data from 28 Atlantic hurricane flights spanning 1982-1993 with a total of 686 dropsondes. In that study, track errors were reduced by 16-30\% in the 12-60-hour lead times by including the dropsonde data.

HRD acquired a Gulfstream IV high-altitude aircraft in 1997, which has been used for hurricane surveillance flights ever since. Several of the earlier data-denial studies drew on dropsonde data collected by that aircraft \citep{Aberson_Franklin1999, Aberson2002, Aberson2010}.

There have been many more recent hurricane-related data-denial studies using operational models and dropsondes from various aircraft platforms: \citet{Aberson2011}, \citet{ChouEA2011}, \citet{MajumdarEA2013}, \citet{ChristophersonEA2017}, \citet{ChristophersonEA2018}, \citet{KrenEA2018}, \citet{TongEA2018}, \citet{AksoyEA2022}, \citet{SippelEA2022}, \citet{DitchekEA2023}, and \citet{SippelEA2026}, to highlight a few. \citet{Majumdar2016} provides a comprehensive review of ``targeted" observations, which is the practice of collecting observations in carefully selected regions around high-impact weather systems. Since collecting aircraft data in hurricanes is fairly costly, data denial studies can help to justify their continued use by demonstrating the improved accuracy they provide to the models.

Although adding data from instruments such as dropsondes generally improves a model, that is not always the case. All data that are collected could have errors that escape quality control checks. In addition, DA schemes are complex and data collected at different times and locations could introduce unexpected interactions and instabilities resulting in a less-than-optimal initial condition for the model. For example, \citet{Aberson2008} provides a documentation of cases during the impactful 2004 and 2005 Atlantic hurricane seasons, in which the forecasts made by an operational model were significantly degraded by the addition of dropsonde data. Although these are extreme examples, they illustrate that, on average, the impact should be positive, but depending on the case, the impact could be neutral or even negative.

While this literature is rich and has consistently documented the remarkable forecast improvements over the last decades, there remains a gap in terms of understanding the economic impacts of these improvements. The question has direct budgetary stakes: the NOAA fleet is being recapitalized at a cost of about \$1.4 billion \citep{GAO2025HurricaneHunter}, and the case for the program rests on the hurricane damages its forecasts help avoid. Here we cover this gap and provide the first economic valuation of a national forecasting asset, the augmented observations taken by the hurricane hunters. We do so by combining the recent data-denial experiments and new available structural approaches to estimate the social value of hazard forecasting improvements.

\section{Model} \label{sec:model}

We follow the cost-loss decision from \citet{molina2024social}, which formalizes how forecast precision translates into avoided social cost. Briefly, an expected-cost-minimizing agent (e.g., a county emergency manager) faces an incoming hurricane of unknown intensity $x$. Before landfall she observes a forecast $\tilde{x}$ and chooses a protective action $a$ at cost $C(a)$. After landfall, intensity is realized and the agent incurs total damage $D(x, a)$, which is increasing in $x$ and decreasing in $a$. The forecast error, $e = x - \tilde{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, has bias $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ that summarizes \emph{ex-ante} forecast uncertainty. Given the forecast, the agent's conditional belief is $x \mid \tilde{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\tilde{x}+\mu,\sigma^2)$, and she solves:
\begin{align}\label{eq:agent_problem}
    \mathcal{C}(\tilde{x};\mu,\sigma) \;=\;
    \min_{a}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,D(x,a(\tilde{x}))\mid \tilde{x}\,\right] + C(a(\tilde{x})).
\end{align}

The \emph{ex-ante} value of a forecast improvement is the reduction in expected minimized total cost from a marginal decrease in $\sigma$, averaged over the distribution of forecasts the agent might receive. \citet{molina2024social} show, given that $e^2$ has variance $2\sigma^4$ under normality, that this value admits a closed-form expression:
\begin{align}\label{eq:voi_marginal}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}}\!\left[\mathcal{C}(\tilde{x};\mu,\sigma)\right] \;=\;
    2\,\sigma\,\cdot\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}}\!\left[\beta_{2}(\tilde{x})\right],
\end{align}
where $\beta_{2}(\tilde{x})$ is the population coefficient from a regression of realized damage $D(x,a^{*}(\tilde{x}))$ on the squared demeaned forecast error $(e-\mu)^{2}$, conditional on the forecast. Intuitively, $\beta_{2}$ measures how strongly damages move with the magnitude of forecast surprise: when this covariance is positive (i.e., damages are convex in errors), tighter forecasts let the agent better align protective spending with the realized threat, and a reduction of $\sigma$ generates positive social value proportional to the baseline level of uncertainty.

The relevant curvature of damages enters as an expectation, $\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}}[\beta_{2}(\tilde{x})]$, taken \emph{ex ante} over the full distribution of forecasts the agent might receive. However, we do not observe multiple realizations of the signal, so  we approximate it by its sample analog and across the forecast realizations we actually observe.

Two implications guide our analysis. First, the marginal value of precision scales linearly with the baseline standard deviation $\sigma$. That is, the same physical improvement in forecast accuracy is worth more when storms are inherently harder to predict. Second, the entire valuation reduces to a single regression coefficient ($\beta_{2}$) and a single physical quantity ($\sigma$), neither of which requires the analyst to observe the protective action, $a$, directly. This is essential in our setting because pre-landfall protective decisions are unobserved, but the cost consequences of their misalignment with realized intensity are recorded in damage and recovery data.

Relevant for our ultimate valuation goal, this framework accommodates any finite improvement in precision that lowers the forecast error standard deviation from a baseline level $\sigma_{0}$ to an improved level $\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{0}$. Integrating \eqref{eq:voi_marginal} over the interval $[\sigma_{1},\,\sigma_{0}]$ yields:
\begin{align}\label{eq:voi_finite}
    V \;=\; \int_{\sigma_{1}}^{\sigma_{0}}
    2\,\sigma\,\beta_{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma \;=\;
    \beta_{2}\,\left(\sigma_{0}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}\right),
\end{align}
so that the social value of moving from a less precise to a more precise forecast is exactly proportional to the reduction in forecast error \emph{variance}. Using a first-order Taylor approximation around the improved baseline, the variance differential can be written as $\sigma_{0}^{2} - \sigma_{1}^{2} \approx 2\,\sigma_{1}\,(\sigma_{0} - \sigma_{1})$. Expression \eqref{eq:voi_finite} demonstrates that the economic value of a physical precision gain scales directly with the baseline level of uncertainty. This structural identity provides the core link that we take to the data, where the precision improvement is the one delivered by augmented observations.

Operationalizing the framework requires three inputs, which structure our empirical analysis: (i) an estimate of $\beta_{2}$ from realized damages and forecast errors (Stage 1); (ii) an estimate of the variance contraction $\sigma_{0}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}$ delivered by the precision improvement (Stage 2); and (iii) a projection of the resulting dollar value across affected counties (Stage 3).

\section{Data} \label{sec:data}

To implement the three-stage procedure outlined in Section \ref{sec:model}, we combine two complementary data sources: an updated version of the economic and operational forecast panel from \citet{molina2024social}, and the data denial experiments of \citet{DitchekEA2023}. Constructing both datasets requires large ensembles of wind and rainfall swaths generated by parametric models informed by NOAA's forecast, the storm's best track (observed path and wind speed of the storm), and the output from the data denial experiments (predictions with and without augmented observations). We describe both relevant datasets below.

\subsection{Economic and Operational Forecast Data}

Estimation of $\beta^{w}_{2}$ draws on the county-by-storm panel constructed in \citet{molina2024social}. Their original dataset covers all 31 Category 1 and greater hurricanes that made landfall in the continental United States between 2005 and 2022. The panel combines two sets of inputs:

First, the official NHC track and intensity forecasts, reconstructed from the contemporaneous operational record and paired with best-track verification, define the ex-ante information available to decision-makers and the ex-post outcomes against which it is evaluated. For each county $c$ and storm $h$, this yields the ex-ante forecast standard deviation $\sigma_{ch}$ and the realized forecast error $e_{ch}$.

Because the ex-ante forecast standard deviation $\sigma_{ch}$ is not observed directly, the panel constructs it by generating a set of 1,000 Monte Carlo synthetic wind and rainfall swaths from parametric wind field and rainfall models for each storm and forecast cycle \citep{lonfat2007parametric, willoughby2006parametric, knaff2007statistical}. These swaths are driven by the official NHC track and intensity forecast, which are then perturbed using the historical NHC forecast-error distribution. The best track record provides the verifying realization against which forecast errors $e_{ch}$ are computed. Across the 31 storms in the panel, this procedure yields a distribution of wind and precipitation swaths, from which county-level forecast and error spreads are then obtained.

Second, the post-landfall costs. These combine direct economic damages from the Spatial Hazard Events and Losses Database for the United States (SHELDUS) with Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) Public Assistance obligations, which capture federally reimbursed recovery activity. Both of these economic series are aggregated to the county level to align with the spatial resolution of the forecast grid.

Here, we augment the original panel with 6 additional Category 1 and greater hurricanes that made landfall in 2023 and 2024. The variables included in the panel remain the same.

We use this panel to estimate $\beta^{w}_{2}$, the marginal damage of a unit increase in the squared demeaned wind speed forecast error. Table \ref{tab:summary_dmg} reports summary statistics for the 37 hurricanes in the sample. The data display substantial heterogeneity across storms in realized wind speed, precipitation, and forecast errors, with total damages and recovery expenditures summing to \$363.7 billion over the period.

\subsection{Data Denial Experiments and Ex-Ante Differentials}

To isolate the contribution of augmented observations, we draw on the data denial experiments of \citet{DitchekEA2023}. The authors rerun forecasts for 92 Atlantic and East Pacific tropical cyclones from the 2017--2020 seasons using the 2020 research-grade, quasi-operational basin-scale configuration of the Hurricane Weather Research and Forecasting (HWRF) model \citep{ZhangEA2016,AlakaEA2022}, with and without the dropsonde observations collected by reconnaissance flights. Forty-one storms had reconnaissance data available; of these, 27 occurred in the Atlantic and eight ultimately made landfall in the continental United States as Category 1 or greater hurricanes (Table \ref{tab:data_denial}). These eight landfalls form our analysis sample for this dataset. Across them, US Air Force and NOAA aircraft collected 49,143 dropsonde measurements, ranging from 184 in Hurricane Hanna to 9,413 in Hurricane Florence.

\input{figures and tables/data_denial}

This sample is well-suited to our valuation exercise for four reasons: i) the large number of model cycles produced for each storm, ii) the dense dropsonde coverage assimilated into each run, iii) the use of a modern operational-grade forecasting system, and iv) the concentration of high-impact US landfalls within a short window. Following \citet{DitchekEA2023}, we denote the configuration that assimilates all available observations as ``ALL'' and the configuration that omits dropsonde data as ``NO.''

The HWRF data denial experiments deliver deterministic intensity and track forecasts at each lead time, but not the spatial distribution of wind and rainfall over affected counties. To recover that distribution, we apply the same parametric wind and rainfall procedure used to construct $\sigma_{ch}$ in the economic panel \citep{lonfat2007parametric, willoughby2006parametric, knaff2007statistical}, this time seeded by the ALL and NO deterministic forecasts in place of the NHC operational forecast.

For each storm and forecast cycle, we generate 1,000 Monte Carlo synthetic swaths under each assimilation regime, perturbing track and intensity according to the historical NHC forecast-error distribution. Across the eight landfalling hurricanes, this procedure yields approximately 84,000 wind and rainfall swaths, from which county-level ensemble spreads $\sigma_{\text{ALL}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{NO}}$ are computed.

We measure the \emph{ex-ante differential} in forecast uncertainty attributable to the reconnaissance network as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_sigma}
    \Delta \sigma_{AO} \;=\; \sigma_{\text{NO}} - \sigma_{\text{ALL}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\text{ALL}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{NO}}$ are the county-level ensemble spreads of the parametric swath distributions seeded by the ALL and NO HWRF configurations, respectively. The quantity $\Delta\sigma_{AO}$ isolates the share of forecast uncertainty resolved by dropsonde observations, which we monetize in Stage 3 by combining it with the loss-function curvature $\hat{\beta}^{w}_{2}$ estimated from the economic panel.

While the institutional definition of AO encompasses an array of aircraft-derived metrics (e.g., flight-level wind vectors, central pressure drops, and tail radar reflectivity), the data denial framework of \citet{DitchekEA2023} isolates the dropsonde channel specifically. Throughout the rest of the paper we therefore use ``AO" as shorthand for the dropsonde-assimilated observation set, and the estimated precision gain $\delta$ should be read as the dropsonde-attributable share of the broader AO system. Consequently, the socioeconomic benefits derived in this study should be interpreted as a conservative lower bound on the aggregate returns to the broader reconnaissance infrastructure.

\section{Empirical Methods} \label{sec:methods}

We operationalize the three-stage framework of Section \ref{sec:model} as follows. Stage 1 estimates the curvature of the loss function from realized damages; Stage 2 estimates the precision gain attributable to AO from the denial experiments; and Stage 3 combines the two into a per-county dollar value.

\subsection{Stage 1: Estimating the Marginal Cost of Forecast Errors}

We first estimate the curvature of the loss function, $\beta$, which maps forecast errors to hurricane costs. Following \citet{molina2024social}, we rely on the assumption that, conditional on realized storm characteristics and fixed effects, the realization of forecast error is quasi-random.

The estimation relies on the following specification, where $c$, $s$, and $h$ index county, state, and hurricane, respectively:
\begin{align} \label{eq:stage1_reg}
    \text{D}_{csh} \quad
        =&
        \quad \beta^w_2 \left(e^w_{csh}-\mu^w_{csh}\right)^2 + \beta^p_2 \left(e^p_{csh}-\mu^p_{csh}\right)^2 \notag\\
        &+ \sum_{b \in \mathcal{E}^w_i} \gamma^w_b 1(x_{csh} \in b) + \sum_{b \in \mathcal{E}^p_i} \gamma^p_b 1(p_{csh} \in b) \notag\\
        & + \gamma_{c} + \eta_{sh} + \varepsilon_{csh}.
\end{align}

Here $\text{D}_{csh}$ represents the total ex-post costs (damages and recovery expenditures). The next expression, $\left(e^w_{csh}-\mu^w_{csh}\right)^2$, is the squared demeaned wind speed forecast error. $x_{csh}$ and $p_{csh}$ are the realized wind speed and precipitation, entered as flexible bins to isolate the cost of the error from the cost of the intensity. $\gamma_{c}$ and $\eta_{sh}$ are county and state-by-hurricane fixed effects.

The coefficient $\hat{\beta}^w_2$ provides the marginal increase in total costs resulting from a unit increase in the demeaned ex-post forecast error. This is the value that will then be scaled by the ex-ante uncertainty of the forecast, which in turn depends on the inclusion of AO.

\subsection{Stage 2: AO and Forecast Precision}

In the second stage, we quantify the physical contribution of AO to forecast precision. We utilize the data denial experiments from \citet{DitchekEA2023} to isolate the reduction in forecast noise attributable to the assimilation of dropsondes.

For each storm $h$ and lead time, we compute the ex-ante error standard deviation (and variance) under two scenarios: the ``No Augmented Observations'' ($\text{NO}$) counterfactual and the ``All Observations'' ($\text{ALL}$) baseline. These are the empirical counterparts of the less- and more-precise forecasts in the model of Section \ref{sec:model}, with $\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{\text{NO}}$ and $\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{\text{ALL}}$. The benefit of the observation network is defined as the reduction in the standard deviation of the forecast error:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \sigma_{h} = \sigma_{\text{NO}, h} - \sigma_{\text{ALL}, h},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\sigma$ represents the standard deviation of the wind prediction spread. A positive $\Delta \sigma_{h}$ indicates that removing the AO increases the dispersion of the forecast signal. To empirically estimate this effect from the data, we use the following regression specification:
\begin{equation}
   \sigma_{csh} = \delta \;\text{AO}_{csh} + \theta \;x_{csh} + \alpha_c + \gamma_h + \varepsilon_{csh},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{csh}$ is the ex-ante wind-speed forecast standard deviation for county $c$ and storm $h$. $\text{AO}_{csh}$ is a dummy that takes a value of 1 when the prediction includes dropsonde profiles (the ``ALL'' configuration of \citet{DitchekEA2023}) and 0 otherwise. The coefficient of interest, $\delta$, identifies the average reduction in forecast noise attributable to the presence of AO($\text{AO}_{csh}$).

$x_{csh}$ is the realized wind speed of the storm to control for the baseline intensity of the hazard across different locations. $\alpha_c$ is a county fixed effect that controls for time-invariant geographic characteristics, $\gamma_h$ is a storm fixed effect that accounts for unobserved, storm-specific heterogeneity, and $\varepsilon_{csh}$ is the idiosyncratic error term. Standard errors are clustered at the county level to allow for arbitrary spatial correlation within jurisdictions. This estimated $\delta$ serves as our empirical measure of $\Delta \sigma$, allowing us to construct the counterfactual uncertainty $\sigma_{\text{NO}} = \sigma_{\text{ALL}} + \delta$ used in the valuation identity \eqref{eq:voi_finite}.

\subsection{Stage 3: Projecting Economic Gains}

In the final stage, we monetize the precision gains identified in Stage 2 by applying the valuation identity in Equation \eqref{eq:voi_finite}. Combining the Stage 1 estimate of the loss-function curvature ($\hat{\beta}^w_{Hurr}$) with the Stage 2 estimate of the precision differential ($\hat{\delta}$), the storm- and county-specific social value of AO is:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:value_calc}
     V^{AO}_{csh} \;=\; \hat{\beta}^w_{Hurr} \,\left(\sigma_{\text{NO},h}^{2} - \sigma_{\text{ALL},h}^{2}\right).
\end{equation}

Following the linear approximation established in Section \ref{sec:model}, this calculation simplifies to $V^{AO}_{csh} \approx 2\,\hat{\beta}^w_{Hurr}\times\sigma_{\text{ALL},h}\times\hat{\delta}$. This operational form explicitly highlights how the final monetary benefits scale with a storm's baseline uncertainty under the active observation regime ($\sigma_{\text{ALL},h}$). Expression \eqref{eq:value_calc} provides our direct, empirical estimate of the avoided damages and optimized protective expenditures attributable solely to the information provided by the dropsonde network. Because the expectation over forecasts is approximated by the signals we observe, the resulting value is conditional on the sampled storms being representative of the broader distribution of landfalling hurricanes; a small or atypical set of signals approximates the \emph{ex-ante} expectation imprecisely.

\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

Following the structure outlined in Section \ref{sec:methods}, we present our findings in three stages: the estimation of the marginal cost of forecast errors, the quantification of the physical forecast improvements attributable to AO, and the final valuation of those improvements.

\subsection{Stage 1: The Marginal Cost of Forecast Errors}

We begin by analyzing the empirical relationship between forecast ex-post error and hurricane costs. Table \ref{tab:voi-table} reports the coefficient estimates for the squared demeaned wind errors across multiple specifications. Columns (1) through (6) show that the coefficient remains positive and statistically significant across various fixed effect strategies, including county-by-month and county-by-year controls.

\input{figures and tables/paper_table_voi}

Column (7) presents our preferred specification, which differentiates exposure to hurricane-force ($\geq33$ m/s) and sub-hurricane-force winds. The empirical results show that the cost of forecast error is driven entirely by high-intensity events. For counties experiencing hurricane-force winds, a one-unit increase in the squared demeaned wind speed error increases total costs (damages plus recovery expenditures) by approximately \$5.03 million (SE = 1.76). In contrast, the effect for sub-hurricane force winds is small and statistically indistinguishable from zero ($\beta = 0.09$).

This finding aligns with the intuition that protective actions and accurate information are most critical when the physical threat is catastrophic. Consequently, for the remainder of the analysis (Stages 2 and 3), we utilize the coefficient $\hat{\beta}^w_{Hurr} = 5.03$ million to value forecast improvements for hurricane-force impacts.

\subsection{Stage 2: AO and Forecast Precision}

In the second stage, we quantify the physical contribution of AO to forecast precision. We utilize the data denial experiments from \citet{DitchekEA2023} to isolate the reduction in forecast noise attributable to the assimilation of dropsondes.

Figure \ref{fig:uncertainty_panels} provides a comprehensive view of the forecast uncertainty environment. The top row characterizes the baseline uncertainty: for hurricane-force events ($\geq33$ m/s), the ex-ante forecast standard deviation ($\sigma$) is significantly higher and grows rapidly as lead time increases (shifting right from 1 to 3 days). This confirms that high-impact storms have a lower signal-to-noise ratio at longer lead times.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures and tables/augmented_panel.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Forecast Uncertainty Dynamics and the Impact of Augmented Observations.} \textit{Top Row:} Kernel density estimates of forecast wind speed ex-ante standard deviation ($\sigma$) by lead time (Red=1 day, Blue=2 days, Green=3 days), stratified by storm intensity. \textit{Bottom Row:} The specific impact on ex-ante standard deviation of AO for hurricane-force winds ($\geq33$ m/s).}
    \label{fig:uncertainty_panels}
\end{figure}

The bottom row isolates the specific contribution of the observation network for these high-impact events. Comparing the distributions with (Purple) and without (Orange) AO reveals a systematic reduction in forecast noise. At the critical 3-day lead time, the \emph{without augmented observations} distribution flattens and extends to the right, indicating a high probability of uncertainty (peaking around 9--10 m/s). In contrast, the assimilation of reconnaissance data shifts the distribution to the left (peaking around 8 m/s) and truncates the right tail.

To formalize the visual pattern in Figure \ref{fig:uncertainty_panels}, we estimate a regression of forecast uncertainty ($\sigma$) on the presence of augmented observations. We restrict the sample to hurricane-force realizations ($\geq33$ m/s) at the 3-day lead time (the lead and intensity at which the social value of the precision gain concentrates, as we establish in Stage 3 below), controlling for observed wind, county-specific exposure, and unobserved storm heterogeneity. The analysis is presented in Table \ref{tab:noaa-controls}. We find that AO reduce the forecast wind speed standard deviation by approximately 0.26 m/s. The estimated $\delta$ is stable across specifications, and suggests the precision gain is not absorbed by county-level exposure or storm-level intensity but instead reflects the information content of the dropsonde data itself.

While 0.26 m/s is modest in absolute terms, it represents a meaningful share of the forecast noise. Compared against all 3-day forecasts in the panel (pooling all storm intensities, mean $\bar{\sigma}_{\text{ALL}} \approx 2.09$ m/s), $\delta$ represents about a 12\% reduction due to AO. When the comparison is restricted to the hurricane-force subsample that drives the valuation in Stage 3 ($\bar{\sigma}_{\text{ALL}} \approx 8.98$ m/s with AO assimilated), $\delta$ represents a reduction of about 2.9\%.

\subsection{Stage 3: Projecting Economic Gains}

Rather than select a lead time and intensity by inspection of Figure \ref{fig:uncertainty_panels}, we compute the implied per-county social value of AO across the full lead-time $\times$ intensity grid. For each cell we estimate the precision gain $\hat{\delta}$ from the Stage 2 specification and translate it into a dollar value, $\hat{\beta}^w_2\,[(\bar{\sigma}_{\text{ALL}}+\hat{\delta})^2 - \bar{\sigma}_{\text{ALL}}^2]$, using the Stage 1 cost parameter for the corresponding intensity (Table \ref{tab:value-surface}; the underlying per-cell regressions are reported in full in Appendix \ref{app:delta}, Table \ref{tab:appB_precision_grid}). The value is identified and maximized in the 3-day hurricane-force cell, at approximately \$23.6 million per county. For sub-hurricane exposures it is negligible, on the order of \$0.01--0.03 million, even though the precision gain is precisely estimated there, because the marginal cost of forecast error is statistically indistinguishable from zero below hurricane force (Table \ref{tab:voi-table}). We therefore adopt the 3-day hurricane-force cell for the headline valuation that follows.

\input{figures and tables/paper_value_surface}

In the final stage, we project the economic value of the observational improvements. Using the results from Stage 2, we found that AO reduce the forecast standard deviation by approximately $\hat{\delta} = 0.26$ m/s at the 3-day lead time. With an average baseline forecast uncertainty ($\bar{\sigma}_{\text{ALL}}$) of approximately 8.98 m/s for the hurricanes in the sample, the 0.26 m/s physical improvement translates to a reduction in variance of roughly 4.7 (m/s)$^2$.\footnote{Recall that the value of a forecast improvement is proportional to the reduction in error variance: $\sigma_{\text{NO}}^{2} - \sigma_{\text{ALL}}^{2} \approx 2\bar{\sigma}_{\text{ALL}}\hat{\delta}$.} Scaling this variance reduction by the marginal cost parameter ($\hat{\beta}^w_{Hurr} = 5.03$ million) yields an estimated benefit of AO of approximately \$23.6 million per county per storm, for counties predicted to be exposed to hurricane-force winds.

We can now directly derive the total value of augmented observations. We note that for the storms with aircraft reconnaissance between 2017 and 2020, only 62 counties were predicted to experience hurricane-force wind speeds. This implies that the total retrospective social value of the AO in this time period is approximately \$1.46 billion.

\subsection{Cost-Benefit Analysis}

For this exercise, we use published official benchmarks of the social return on NOAA's hurricane research aircraft platform. The main source is the 2016 Analysis of Alternatives (AoA) commissioned by NOAA's Office of Marine and Aviation Operations \citep{ANSER2016ExternalBenefits}. That study quantified four direct benefit channels, all reported in net present value (NPV) terms. Namely, these were the reduction of needless evacuations (\$138.84 million), commercial air traffic disruption (\$134.18M), commercial maritime traffic disruption (\$0.18M), and benefits to atmospheric chemistry research (\$227.65 million). Their calculations indicate a total NPV of \$500.86 million (base-year \$2024, $r = 7\%$) over the 25-year operating life of an observation platform. The life-cycle cost (LCC) of the least-cost alternative considered (two modified Lockheed C-130J aircraft) was \$680 million NPV over the same horizon, yielding a base-case benefit-to-cost ratio of approximately 0.75:1. The authors explicitly noted that this likely understates the total societal return, since the analysis considered only direct over-prediction cost-avoidance, omitted indirect impacts, and adopted uniformly conservative parameter values.

Our framework captures a benefit channel that the 2016 AoA does not address: the social value of ex ante variance reduction in the wind-speed forecast, monetized through a cost function estimated from realized hurricane damages \citep{molina2024social}. On the 2017--2020 retrospective denial sample, this single channel yields a social value of approximately \$1.46 billion or roughly 2.9 times the entire 2030--2055 NPV that the AoA estimated, despite covering only four storm seasons and abstracting from precipitation, surge, and energy-sector mechanisms. Annualized over the four years of data-denial experiments, the implied benefit rate is approximately \$366 million per year, against an AoA-implied benefit accrual of \$20 million per year over the replacement platform's forward operating life. The two figures are not strictly comparable, however, as one is a forward NPV of avoided costs from continued operations, while the other is a retrospective valuation of the information content already delivered by past missions. The order-of-magnitude gap nevertheless illustrates that the official benchmark, by construction, omits the largest channel through which this platform generates social value.

The implications for the ongoing recapitalization of the NOAA aircraft fleet are stark. The 2022 NOAA Aircraft Recapitalization Plan \citep{NOAA2022AircraftPlan} initiated the procurement of four C-130J aircraft to replace the aging WP-3D fleet, with a total acquisition cost subsequently estimated at approximately \$1.4 billion \citep{GAO2025HurricaneHunter}, of which roughly \$678 million remained to be funded as of January 2025. The retrospective social value from a single four-year window of operations under our framework (\$1.46 billion) recovers the entire undiscounted acquisition cost of the replacement fleet, and exceeds the remaining unfunded balance by more than a factor of two. Even under the AoA's own most-conservative LCC point estimate of \$680M NPV over 25 years, our retrospective valuation alone recovers the full lifecycle cost more than twice over. Importantly, our \$1.46 billion headline figure isolates the \emph{dropsonde-attributable} channel of aircraft reconnaissance. Because other onboard sensors, such as flight-level wind vectors, central-pressure estimates, and tail radar reflectivity, are held constant across both the ALL and NO configurations, our valuation should be interpreted as a conservative lower bound on the aggregate social value delivered by the NOAA aircraft infrastructure.

\section{Conclusion and Discussion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This paper provides a novel empirical valuation of augmented aircraft observations in the context of hurricane forecasting. By linking data denial experiments to a structural model of hurricane costs, we quantify the socioeconomic return on in-situ meteorological data collection. Our results demonstrate that the assimilation of dropsonde data from the NOAA aircraft fleet significantly improves forecast precision, which in turn generates substantial social value by reducing both avoidable damages and misallocated protective expenditures.

Our analysis proceeds in three stages. First, we establish that the social cost of forecast uncertainty is highly sensitive to intensity, with a marginal cost parameter that is statistically significant only for hurricane-force events. Second, we show that AO reduce forecast standard deviation by approximately 0.26 m/s at the critical 3-day lead time. Finally, we project these physical gains into monetary terms. With an average baseline forecast uncertainty of approximately 8.98 m/s for counties predicted to experience hurricane-force winds, the 0.26 m/s prediction gain translates into a benefit of approximately \$23.6 million per county per storm. Aggregated across the 62 affected counties in our 2017--2020 retrospective denial sample, the total social value reaches \$1.46 billion. This single four-year window of benefits effectively recovers the entire \$1.4 billion acquisition cost of NOAA's planned C-130J fleet recapitalization, providing powerful empirical justification for sustained capital modernization.

\subsection{Policy Implications}

Our findings carry several implications for how NOAA evaluates and operates its reconnaissance program. First, because the economic value of AO is identified from data-denial output, we recommend institutionalizing routine data-denial experiments for \emph{all} landfalling US hurricanes, rather than treating them as isolated research exercises. A continuous counterfactual record would let NOAA track the marginal contribution of dropsondes storm by storm and help optimize flight patterns and deployment strategies. Second, performance reporting should be augmented with ex ante metrics. Traditional verification emphasizes \emph{ex post} track and intensity error, but it is the reduction in ex ante ensemble spread ($\Delta\sigma^2$) that drives protective decision-making and hence social value; reporting it alongside conventional error tracks would tie missions directly to their economic return. Third, capital-justification analyses such as the Aircraft Recapitalization Plan should adopt a structural valuation anchored in observed precision gains rather than assumption-based ``value chain'' scenarios. Finally, we recommend disseminating these valuations through a routine, biennial socioeconomic impact report so that program funding remains commensurate with its contribution to national economic resilience.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}

Our estimates likely represent a lower bound. The framework focuses on wind-speed-driven costs and does not explicitly account for the value of improved precipitation or storm surge forecasts, nor does it capture secondary benefits to the maritime and aviation sectors identified in NOAA's external benefit reports. Future research should aim to integrate these multi-hazard dimensions to provide an even more comprehensive account of the value of weather information. Ultimately, this study underscores that in the face of increasing hurricane intensity and coastal exposure, the information provided by AO is one of the most high-leverage tools available for enhancing national economic resilience.

\section{Robustness of the Marginal Cost Parameter} \label{app:beta}

Here we assess the stability of the Stage 1 estimate of $\hat{\beta}^w_{Hurr}$ along two dimensions. First, we cover the choice of outcome variable. We take this as evidence that no single storm drives the parameter; Katrina and Michael are the most influential observations, but removing either one leaves the headline coefficient well within an economically meaningful range.

\section{Robustness of the Precision Gain} \label{app:delta}

Here we assess the stability of the Stage 2 estimate of the augmented-observations precision gain $\delta$ along four dimensions. First, we report the full per-cell precision regressions that underlie the value surface of Table \ref{tab:value-surface}, estimated across the lead-time $\times$ intensity grid with the realized-wind control, fixed effects, and goodness-of-fit statistics (Table \ref{tab:appB_precision_grid}). For hurricane-force realizations the precision gain $\delta$ grows monotonically in lead time, from a small and statistically insignificant 0.063 m/s at the 1-day lead to a statistically significant 0.258 m/s at the 3-day lead; for sub-hurricane realizations $\delta$ is small at every lead. These results indicate that the value of AO is concentrated at operationally relevant horizons and intensities, and that they deliver the largest ex-ante precision gains when forecast uncertainty is greatest.

Second, we test for sensitivity across exposure to different Saffir-Simpson hurricane categories on land.\footnote{Here it is important to note that Saffir-Simpson categorization is given to hurricanes \emph{over water} based on the maximum sustained winds near the eye of the storm. This implies that a Category 5 hurricane over water, for example, will have winds of lesser speed distributed on land. All of our measurements are for winds experienced \emph{on land}.} The results are shown in Table \ref{tab:appB_intensity}. The table shows that the precision gain is identified at Category 1 and above but attenuates and loses statistical significance at higher-category cut-offs. Category 4 and above cannot be estimated because the denial sample contains fewer than 10 such observations, and no Category 5 exposures are represented in the data. The results indicate that the headline precision gain is anchored on Category 1 and above forecasts, where the continental-US denial sample is large enough to identify $\delta$.

Third, we test for sensitivity to individual storms. A one-storm-out jackknife across the six hurricane-force denials at the 3-day lead shows that the precision gain is heavily concentrated in Hurricane Irma (Table \ref{tab:appB_jackknife}). Dropping Irma collapses $\hat{\delta}$ from 0.258 m/s to 0.035 m/s and renders it statistically indistinguishable from zero, while dropping any of the other storms does not meaningfully attenuate our results.

Fourth, we consider the inclusion of Hurricane Maria. While Maria is excluded from the main analysis due to its landfall in Puerto Rico, its denial archive contributes 150 additional hurricane-force observations at the 3-day lead—more than the entire continental-US sample combined. Adding Maria back roughly doubles the panel, raises the precision-gain estimate to 0.356 m/s, and reduces the standard error by approximately 50\% (Table \ref{tab:appB_maria}). Crucially, integrating Maria resolves the small-sample limitations identified in the previous checks. Because Puerto Rico is a relatively small landmass, storms experience less surface friction than during continental landfalls, yielding the substantial Category 3 and Category 4 overland exposures that the continental-US sample lacks.

When working with this expanded sample, the precision gain grows monotonically with storm intensity across all four statistically significant category cut-offs (Table \ref{tab:appB_intensity_maria}). Furthermore, the larger sample renders the estimate robust to the exclusion of any single storm, including Irma (Table \ref{tab:appB_jackknife_maria}). We interpret these findings as evidence that the fragility documented in the baseline continental-US sample is a small-sample artifact rather than a weakness of the precision-gain identification itself.

\section{Per-Storm Decomposition of the Retrospective Social Value} \label{app:per-storm}

Here we decompose the \$1.46 billion retrospective social value of AO across the 2017--2020 denial sample by individual storm. Per-county value equals the marginal cost parameter times the variance reduction, $\hat{\beta}^{w}_{Hurr} \times \Delta\sigma^{2} \approx \$23.6$ million summed across the affected hurricane-force / 3-day-lead county observations of each storm (Table \ref{tab:appC_per_storm}). We organize the discussion along three dimensions.

First, the distribution of contributing storms. Six of the eight 2017--2020 landfalling hurricanes with denial experiments in the continental-US archive contribute to the retrospective valuation. Hurricanes Harvey and Hanna do not appear because they did not produce hurricane-force wind realizations at the 3-day lead in the denial sample. The implication is that the headline retrospective figure is identified from storms with substantial hurricane-force exposure at the operational lead time, not from a broad cross-section of weak landfalls.

Second, concentration in the top contributors. Hurricane Michael (2018) generates \$638 million (43.5\% of the total), and Hurricane Laura (2020) generates \$307 million (21.0\%), reflecting their large hurricane-force footprints at the 3-day lead (27 and 13 affected counties, respectively). The remaining four storms (Irma, Sally, Florence, Isaias) jointly account for the remaining 35\%. The implication is that the headline aggregate is heavily weighted by the two largest landfalls in the sample.

Third, the source of the concentration. The dominance of Michael and Laura is partially mechanical. Every affected county-storm in the denial sample contributes the same per-county valuation of \$23.6 million, so a storm's total value scales linearly with its hurricane-force / 3-day-lead county count. Michael's 27 counties and Laura's 13 simply reflect the geographic breadth of their hurricane-force impacts.

\subsection*{Extending Beyond the Denial Archive}

The \$1.46 billion headline is identified from the six storms with hurricane-force exposure in the 2017--2020 denial archive, yet augmented observations are flown for essentially every hurricane threatening the continental United States. The denial sample therefore captures only a fraction of the value the program has delivered. Beyond simply covering more events, the broader panel sharpens the valuation itself: because the social value is an \emph{ex-ante} expectation over potential forecasts that we approximate by averaging across observed signals, a larger and more representative set of forecasts brings that sample average closer to the population expectation it stands in for. To gauge the full magnitude, we extend the same valuation to every hurricane-force, 3-day-lead county-storm in the 2005--2024 operational forecast panel. Each county-storm is now priced at its own baseline forecast uncertainty $\sigma_3$ rather than the pooled sample average, holding the marginal cost parameter and the precision gain fixed at their Stage 1 and Stage 2 estimates, so that $V = \hat{\beta}^{w}_{Hurr}\,[(\sigma_3+\delta)^2 - \sigma_3^2]$. As in the denial sample, the 3-day-lead hurricane-force criterion excludes rapidly forming storms with no operational hurricane-force forecast at that horizon.

Table \ref{tab:extended_valuation} reports the results. Applying storm-specific uncertainty to the same six denial storms reproduces the 62 county-storms at \$1.42 billion, within three percent of the \$1.46 billion headline that applies the pooled baseline uniformly. Extended across the full panel, 27 storms produce 216 hurricane-force county-storms and a cumulative retrospective social value of approximately \$5.2 billion over 2005--2024, or \$3.9 billion over the more recent and better-observed 2010--2024 window. Both windows imply an annualized benefit of roughly \$259 million per year, consistent with the \$366 million per year implied by the smaller but hurricane-force-heavy denial sample. The broader panel thus reinforces the central finding: even a single channel of the reconnaissance program, the dropsonde-attributable reduction in wind-speed forecast variance, generates social value that comfortably exceeds the cost of the aircraft infrastructure that produces it.
